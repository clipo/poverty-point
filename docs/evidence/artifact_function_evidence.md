# Artifact Function Evidence

## Key Questions
- What functions did Poverty Point objects (PPOs) serve, and does their standardized form reflect social rather than purely functional purposes?
- What were plummets used for, and does their exotic material composition reflect display value or functional requirements?
- How do material selection patterns inform the costly signaling argument?

## Evidence

### From Pierce 1997
- PPOs were made by hand-rolling clay in 7-13 seconds per object; "the five most common forms, consisting of 88 percent of the assemblage, differ only in one production step" (pp. 164, 171)
- PPO size (50-80 g) was constrained by thermal performance (heat transfer vs. heat storage optimization), but "the wide variety of shapes appear to have no impact on performance" (pp. 164, 178)
- PPOs are a convergent technological solution found in multiple regions (lower Mississippi Valley, Australia, California's Central Valley), undermining their use as cultural boundary markers (p. 167)
- Chi-square tests show significant differences in PPO type frequencies across different collection areas within the Poverty Point site, potentially reflecting spatial heterogeneity from different occupying groups (p. 181)
- The Poverty Point site alone contributed 17,923 PPOs across all form types in the analyzed assemblage (p. 180, Table 11.2)
- PPO form diversity was highest during the peak period of site occupation, declining after pottery adoption (p. 178)

### From Hays 2018
- Experimental evidence: "systematic thermal and cooking experiments indicate that heating and cooking with simple clay lumps is a more efficient technology than the relatively complex forms used by Poverty Point people" (p. 9)
- Pierce (1998) concluded "the wide variety of shapes appear to have no impact on performance"; McGee's (1995) experiments showed "amorphous or round forms work just as well (or perhaps better) at retaining and releasing heat" (p. 9)
- "PPOs have been found in areas with abundant river gravels and at sites with abundant fire-cracked rock," refuting the hypothesis that PPOs were simply substitutes for cooking stones (p. 9)
- PPO types are geographically patterned: "biconical PPOs appear to be strongly associated with people north of the LMV; cross-grooved and cylindrical-grooved PPOs are strongly associated with the LMV and the Gulf Coast" (p. 12)
- At Poverty Point and Claiborne, all six main PPO types are present; sites with all types are aggregation centers where multiple groups converged (p. 12)
- All six main types are present from lowest to uppermost levels at Poverty Point, ruling out temporal change as an explanation for type variation (p. 9)
- Unusual PPOs are "frequently elaborately decorated" and "often are not oxidized through repeated firings," suggesting they were not used for cooking (p. 10)
- Gibson (1973) estimated Poverty Point holds approximately 15 million PPOs (p. 7)
- After the collapse of Poverty Point, "hematite and magnetite plummets essentially disappear from the Southeast" (p. 2)
- Comparative data (Table 1): Poverty Point has 2,790 plummets; 11,714 projectile points; 1,214 stone beads; ca. 30,000 microliths; 702 galena objects; 2,221 steatite objects (p. 2)

### From Lipo 2012
- Morphometric analysis of 78 plummets: width is strongly constrained while length varies freely, inconsistent with fishing weights, bola stones, or charm stones (pp. 87-88)
- Plummet hafting features are minimal: perforations only ca. 2.0 mm; grooves less than 1 mm deep (pp. 88-89)
- No use-wear, impact damage, or mid-shaft breakage patterns consistent with throwing or fishing use (p. 89)
- Plummets are consistently found in domestic/habitation contexts, not fishing or hunting contexts (p. 88)
- Both grooved and perforated plummets show identical shape constraints, suggesting a shared functional context (p. 87)
- Hematite (specific gravity 5.3) and magnetite (specific gravity 5.15) have nearly identical densities, allowing consistent weight in standardized dimensions, consistent with warp-weighted loom weights (p. 89)
- Direct evidence of Archaic-age textile production exists from Arnold Research Cave, Missouri (3025-2440 B.C.) and Avery Island, Louisiana (2900-2460 B.C.) (p. 90)
- Over 2,300 plummets found at Poverty Point, with approximately 80% hematite, 20% magnetite, and a few of other materials (p. 85)
- Plummets are restricted to the Late Archaic and "essentially disappear" afterward, consistent with technological replacement rather than loss of subsistence function (p. 90)

### From Blackburn 2003
- Hematite hardness 5.5-6.5 on the Mohs scale; 70% metallic iron; magnetite similarly hard at 73.4% iron (pp. 24-25)
- Drilling of plummets done from one side using a "swamp cane drill," requiring substantial labor to penetrate hard iron minerals (pp. 25-26)
- Material-form correlation: "drilled or perforated plummets from Mississippi and Louisiana sites are almost exclusively fashioned from magnetite, while the grooved plummets are almost exclusively made from hematite" (p. 26)
- Plummets found as burial offerings as well as in domestic contexts, suggesting dual utilitarian and symbolic functions (p. 26)

## Synthesis

The artifact evidence reveals a consistent pattern: objects at Poverty Point exhibit non-functional elaboration that exceeds what utilitarian performance requires. For PPOs, experimental evidence demonstrates that shape does not affect cooking performance and that simple amorphous clay lumps are equally or more efficient. Yet Poverty Point populations invested additional steps (7-13 seconds per object in five standardized forms rather than 2 seconds for amorphous lumps) in producing forms that served no thermal advantage. The geographic patterning of PPO types (biconical associated with populations north of the lower Mississippi Valley, cross-grooved with the core region) supports the interpretation that PPO forms encoded group identity information visible during aggregation. The co-occurrence of all types at Poverty Point and Claiborne, the two largest aggregation sites, is predicted by Conkey's (1980) model that aggregation sites should display the greatest stylistic diversity.

For plummets, the evidence is more complex. Lipo et al.'s (2012) morphometric analysis provides the strongest functional argument: the asymmetric constraint (width standardized, length free) is inconsistent with weights, bola stones, or ornaments, but consistent with warp-weighted loom weights. This reinterpretation suggests that hematite and magnetite were selected for their physical density properties (enabling small, heavy weights) rather than for visual display. However, the 134 decorated plummets in Webb's sample and the use of plummets as burial offerings at other sites complicate this purely functional reading. The material selection itself remains a costly behavior: hematite and magnetite from Arkansas (~250 km) had to be acquired through exchange and were extremely labor-intensive to shape given their hardness (5.5-6.5 on the Mohs scale, requiring organic tools to drill perforations of only 2 mm).

Whether plummets functioned as loom weights, ornaments, or both, their exotic material composition and the labor required to produce them make them genuinely costly artifacts. The key distinction is between PPOs (cheap, locally produced, socially encoded cooking implements) and plummets/beads/gorgets (costly, imported, labor-intensive signal goods). This distinction maps onto the costly signaling framework: everyday activities used low-cost, locally available materials, while signaling involved investment in difficult-to-obtain, labor-intensive exotic materials.

## Gaps
- No loom remains have been found at Poverty Point, leaving the loom weight hypothesis for plummets without direct supporting evidence
- Use-wear analysis of PPOs has not been systematically conducted to confirm or refute cooking versus other functions
- Whether decorated/unusual PPOs served as exchange tokens or identity markers remains speculative
- The relationship between PPO type proportions and group identity has not been tested quantitatively
- No experimental study has tested the loom weight hypothesis with replica plummets and frame
- The decorative/symbolic dimension of plummets (134 decorated specimens) is not accounted for by the loom weight hypothesis
