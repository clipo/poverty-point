# Signaling Theory Evidence

## Key Questions
- What theoretical frameworks explain why costly, non-reproductive behaviors (monument construction, exotic goods acquisition) can be adaptive?
- Does the archaeological evidence from Poverty Point match the predictions of costly signaling and the "waste" hypothesis?
- What parallels exist between Poverty Point and other cases of costly cultural elaboration?

## Evidence

### From Dunnell and Greenlee 1999
- The "waste" hypothesis: "under some specific selective conditions (e.g., unpredictably variable or 'marginal' environments) the diversion of energy into nonreproductive activity (e.g., cultural elaborations termed 'waste'), necessarily at the expense of reproduction, might become fixed in some populations at relatively high levels" (p. 381)
- Corollary: "increases in the environmental carrying capacity (or in its predictability) will decrease the fitness of those cultural elaborations" (p. 381)
- An "increase in carrying capacity would select against high levels of waste. Increases in net productivity might arise in one or both of two broadly distinct ways: climatic change toward warmer and/or wetter conditions or a change in subsistence productivity" (p. 381)
- Evidence from the Hopewell case: the "collapse" of elaborate cultural practices "correlated with climatic amelioration, not deterioration" (p. 386)
- "The particular form that waste takes within a population, in this case earthworks, mounds, mortuary features, grave goods, and so on, is a matter of history and transmission (i.e., homology)" (p. 381)
- "Several of the social and/or political hypotheses can be eliminated from consideration as potential explanations of the Hopewell 'collapse' because their empirical implications were not met. There did not appear to be an increase in indicators of warfare" (p. 388)
- "We anticipate that population size should increase as waste is reduced" because "when energy is no longer channeled away from reproduction, fertility rates will increase" (p. 381)
- The Hopewell "collapse" was "not entirely a unilinear path. Different forms of waste persisted differentially" (p. 388)
- Middle Woodland Hopewell exotic materials included marine shells, mica, quartz crystals, grizzly bear teeth, obsidian, pottery, galena, copper, silver, and chalcedony, paralleling Poverty Point's exchange inventory (p. 377)

### From Hamilton and Buchanan 2009
- "Flintknapping may also have been a form of costly signal" because "Clovis projectile point technology is complex and would have required a significant amount of investment both in terms of time and energy to learn effectively" (p. 67)
- The accumulated copying error (ACE) model shows that "continuous traits passed down across generations through social learning will drift deterministically over time due to the multiplicative nature of the accumulation of copying errors" (p. 60)
- Biased transmission (conformist or prestige bias) constrains variance: "the amount of variation within a population should stabilize through time at a level determined by the strength of bias, lambda" (p. 60)
- Empirically measured copying error rate (Weber fraction) for Clovis points: sigma_e = 0.063 (5.3%); bias parameter lambda = 0.38, indicating about 1 in every ~2.6 transmission events involved copying a prestigious individual (pp. 64-66)
- "Copying error constitutes about one quarter of the variance in Clovis projectile point size" (p. 65)
- The BACE model "is the appropriate null model for the evolution of continuous traits over time" (p. 67)

### From Hays 2018
- "Feasting establishes ties between groups near and far, and this creates alliances that act as social safety networks" (p. 5)
- Feasting in transegalitarian societies: "(1) large and successful feasts help cement and maintain alliances; (2) feasts are often used to attract and support communal labor for projects; (3) crafts are often produced by and for the community in preparation for feasts" (p. 6)
- Ortmann and Kidder (2013) "estimate that it [Mound A] was built in about three months with a workforce ranging between 1,000 and 3,000 laborers plus their families" (p. 6)
- Five of Hayden's feasting signatures are present at Poverty Point: unusual preparation/serving vessels (steatite), prestige items, ritualized paraphernalia, and ritualized items for smoking (p. 6)
- "The number of PPOs at Poverty Point suggest, however, that the feasts hosted there were much larger and more numerous than at any other Late/Terminal Archaic site" (p. 11)
- Non-utilitarian items "are widely distributed across the site ridges, suggesting that no person or group had control over their use" (p. 11)
- "Interestingly, it appears that their value drops dramatically after the 'collapse' of the Poverty Point culture around 1000 BC, because many of these items are either very rare or not present in Early Woodland/Tchula period sites" (p. 11)

### From Kidder and Grooms 2025
- "Between 4000 and 3000 cal BP, the Lower Mississippi Valley experienced significant climatic and environmental changes, including increased rainfall, cooler temperatures, and high-frequency flooding" (p. 1)
- "Indigenous peoples responded to these disruptions by coming together at the Poverty Point site" to perform rituals and construct earthworks (p. 1)
- "Poverty Point was occupied over a shorter time than previously asserted, and the earthworks were built rapidly over a span of months (or just a few years) by a large voluntary labor force drawn from across eastern North America" (p. 1)
- "In the absence of compulsory labor, we see this as evidence that the community agreed that there was an urgent moral requirement to expend such effort" (p. 11)
- "Over 10,000 projectile points from Poverty Point, many made on lithic material that is found hundreds of kilometers distant, encompassing stylistic diversity beyond that commonly found at contemporary or earlier sites" (p. 7)
- "Poverty Point is at the extreme margin of known human behavior within both forager societies worldwide and Native American pre-Invasion societies" (p. 11)
- The authors explicitly reject several alternative frameworks: "not attempts to distract from reproduction, symbols of power, displays of magic, or ritualized economic practices" (p. 12)
- Material flows into Poverty Point but does not visibly flow out, inconsistent with trade-center models (p. 7)
- Revitalization movements are "usually stimulated by people's recognition of a critical situation or decline, which is perceived to demand urgent action" (p. 6)
- Sodality membership requires "novel material expressions such as dress, icons, dances, songs, architecture, and body modifications that signal membership and enhance a sense of group unity" (p. 7)

### From Hawkes 2000

- Hadza hunter daily success rate is .034 prey/hunter; geometric mean of weeklong runs is essentially zero. "With this measure, the expected provisioning payoff for big-game hunting is nothing at all" (p. 65). Establishes the variance argument that destroys the provisioning interpretation of hunting and grounds the costly-signaling reading in ethnographic data.
- Better Hadza hunters spend MORE time hunting, not less; their wives work MORE, not less; their children's nutritional improvement runs through wives' foraging effort, not through fathers' meat shares (pp. 64, 67). The most surprising empirical finding for the costly-signaling argument: better hunters do not benefit their families through their hunting *directly*, supporting the competitive-display reading over the family-provisioning model.
- Hadza hunter household keeps ≤10% of own kills; the carcass is not the hunter's property in any practical sense (pp. 63-64). The "demand sharing" pattern (p. 63, citing Peterson 1993) supports tolerated-theft / costly-signaling readings over reciprocal-altruism readings.
- Big-game hunting is a competitive arena: better hunters are valuable resources, hunting prowess "may be among a man's most important characteristics" (p. 71), and partner choice favors better hunters. The chapter is qualitative on the partner-choice premium; no specific reproductive-success number is stated. (Verification note: Paper 1's "10-20% reproductive success advantage" attribution to Hawkes 2000 is not present in this chapter; see `claims/hawkes_2000_claims.md` Verification Notes.)
- Hadza prey size ~30× chimpanzee prey size yields the same individual hunter success rate (.034 vs .037) but vastly larger consumable bonanzas, creating the conditions for sharing-as-display (p. 70). Relevant to the PP project as a parallel mechanism: large unpredictable bonanzas attract many consumers, making sharing inevitable and creating the social arena in which costly-signaling distinctions can register.

### From Wiessner 2002

- 34-year longitudinal Ju/'hoansi (!Kung) dataset evaluating four hypotheses (reciprocity, costly signaling, nepotism, long-term political goals) for why men hunt large game. Wiessner's overall conclusion is non-reductive: all four benefits accrue, with relative importance shifting over a man's life (Abstract; p. 429 Conclusions).
- Costly signaling supports first marriage but not enduring polygyny: "While signaling hidden abilities through large-game hunting appears to be an important factor for at least first marriage, once bride service has been completed, there is little indication that signaling via hunting success played a role in men outcompeting other men for mates" (p. 421). Refines the costly-signaling-as-mate-acquisition reading.
- Good hunters' families have substantially more hxaro partners (mean 42.9 vs 23.7, p=.006), more household possessions (60.4 vs 40.7, p=.02), more distant-kin hxaro partners (27.6 vs 11.4, p=.004), longer camp-residence stability (32.0 vs 17.4 yrs, p=.0001), and more co-resident adult married children (84% vs 31%, p=.03) than poor hunters' families (Tables 2-4, pp. 422-426). Quantifies a meaningful but variable network-access premium: 80% more hxaro partners, 48% more possessions, 142% more distant-kin partnerships, 84% longer camp residence, 171% more adult-children co-residence.
- Hxaro provides ~93% of camps visited during food shortages and conflicts (p. 422) and ~69% of household possessions (p. 422). Hxaro is the dominant shortfall-buffering and material-flow mechanism, not a marginal supplement.
- Large-game hunting provided more than 80% of the diet during "hungry months" in the Nyae Nyae area 1996-1998 (p. 416, citing Wiessner & N!aici 1998). Establishes that big-game is the dominant calorie source during shortfalls in arid Kalahari conditions.
- Average Ju/'hoansi adult has 16 hxaro partners (range 2-42); 18% same camp, 24% in 1-15 km camps, 25% in 16-50 km camps, 33% in 51-200 km camps (p. 421). Provides directly modelable network-parameterization data for the framework's obligation-network module. (Verification note: Paper 1 line 147's "10-30% additional resources during shortfalls" attribution to Wiessner 2002 is not present in this paper; see `claims/wiessner_2002_claims.md` Verification Notes. The actual hxaro-shortfall-buffering numbers are 69-93%, not 10-30%.)

## Synthesis

Three theoretical frameworks converge on explaining Poverty Point's monumental construction and exotic goods acquisition as adaptive costly behavior under environmental uncertainty. Dunnell and Greenlee's (1999) "waste" hypothesis provides the evolutionary mechanism: under unpredictable environmental conditions, diversion of energy into non-reproductive cultural elaboration (earthworks, exotic goods, ritual) can be adaptive because it buffers populations against resource crises. The corollary prediction, that such elaboration will be selected against when conditions improve, maps onto the Hopewell collapse (which correlated with climatic amelioration) and potentially onto the Poverty Point collapse as well, though the Poverty Point case may involve environmental deterioration beyond the threshold where cooperation was viable rather than improvement below the threshold where it was needed.

Hamilton and Buchanan's (2009) cultural transmission framework provides the methodological apparatus for analyzing artifact variation within the signaling system. Their finding that complex craft production (Clovis points) can function as costly signals in hunter-gatherer contexts establishes a precedent for interpreting the labor-intensive production of exotic artifacts at Poverty Point. The quantified parameters (Weber fraction of ~5%, bias parameter of 0.38) offer baselines for analyzing whether PPO and plummet variation at Poverty Point reflects neutral drift, biased transmission, or selection.

Hays's (2018) feasting framework operationalizes the social context in which costly signaling occurred. Large-scale, ritualized feasts during aggregation events provided the arena for displaying costly investments: the host community's capacity to construct earthworks, accumulate exotic materials, and provision large numbers of visitors. The archaeological signatures of feasting (massive PPO production, steatite serving vessels, ritual paraphernalia distributed across the site) are present at Poverty Point. Importantly, the "value" of prestige items collapsed after the Poverty Point system ended, suggesting their worth was maintained by the signaling system itself rather than being intrinsic.

Kidder and Grooms (2025) provide the most recent and empirically grounded interpretation: Poverty Point was constructed rapidly by voluntary, geographically diverse labor during a period of climatic disruption. Their emphasis on voluntary participation, authority-based (not coercive) leadership, and environmental crisis as the trigger aligns with costly signaling predictions, even though the authors frame their argument in terms of revitalization and moral obligation rather than evolutionary ecology. The behavioral pattern they describe (aggregation, costly cooperative investment, costly material deposition during environmental stress) matches the signaling model's predictions regardless of the interpretive vocabulary used.

The key empirical predictions of the signaling framework that are supported by the Poverty Point evidence include: (1) costly investment under environmental uncertainty (monumental construction coincident with Neoglacial climate disruption); (2) honest signals that cannot be easily faked (physically massive earthworks requiring large cooperative labor, exotic materials from distant sources); (3) no centralized coercion (absent hierarchy, elite residences, and wealth concentration); (4) signal value dependent on the system (exotic goods lose value after the system collapses); and (5) aggregation as the context for signaling (diverse projectile point traditions, all PPO types, and wide-catchment raw materials at the type-site).

## Gaps
- No formal test has been conducted to distinguish waste/signaling from alternative explanations (prestige competition, redistribution, pilgrimage) using the Poverty Point data
- The environmental conditions at Poverty Point's collapse (ca. 3100 cal BP) differ from the Hopewell case: Poverty Point may have ended due to extreme environmental deterioration rather than amelioration, which is the opposite of the waste hypothesis prediction
- The individual-level benefits of participating in the signaling system (enhanced mating success, risk insurance, information access) have not been quantified or tested
- The relationship between the intensity of signaling (construction labor, exotic accumulation) and the severity of environmental uncertainty has not been empirically calibrated
- Whether the signaling was primarily group-level (monument construction) or individual-level (exotic goods display) remains unclear
- Cross-cultural comparison with Rapa Nui and Chaco Canyon within the costly signaling framework has not yet been formally conducted for Poverty Point
