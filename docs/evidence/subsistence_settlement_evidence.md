# Subsistence and Settlement Evidence

## Key Questions
- What was the subsistence base of Poverty Point populations, and could it support aggregation without agriculture?
- Was the site permanently occupied or seasonally aggregated?
- What settlement system structure connected the main site to satellite communities?

## Evidence

### From Jackson 1981a
- Faunal analysis at J.W. Copes site (13 km from Poverty Point) shows deer and fish dominating the assemblage, with selective procurement rather than broad-spectrum opportunistic foraging (p. 80)
- Seasonal indicators: fish vertebrae annuli indicate mainly summer collection; deer abundance suggests fall/winter; a shed-antler skull indicates spring; nuts and Chenopodium indicate late summer/fall (pp. 80, 84)
- Collectively, evidence "does point to the possibility of a year-round occupation" at this peripheral site (p. 84)
- Floral remains include hickory/pecan, acorn, chenopodium, cane, wild bean, and abundant wood charcoal (p. 80)
- Even this small (0.5 ha) peripheral site participated in long-distance exchange: one cube of galena from southeastern Missouri, approximately 800 km distant (p. 80)
- The site has a 1.0 m thick midden with 980 artifacts from a single test unit, suggesting sustained occupation (pp. 77, 79)

### From Jackson 1986
- Systematic flotation from Poverty Point features: hickory nutshells and acorn dominated, with hickory in 30 of 35 samples and acorn in 21 samples; 7,778 acorn parts versus 3,712 hickory nutshell fragments (p. 68)
- Only one specimen each of Polygonum and Chenopodium, suggesting cultigens played "a minor role at best in the diet" (p. 68)
- Thomas and Campbell (1978) suggest "the nut masts on Macon Ridge may have provided a suitably productive resource to promote a seasonal aggregation of people" (pp. 73-74)
- Jackson argues a "sedentary hunter-gatherer population of any substantial size at Poverty Point would have quickly led to environmental degradation," supporting seasonal aggregation (p. 39, citing Jackson 1986:537 via Ortmann)
- Inter-group networks "become extended by the formation of alliances through marriage, trade, or ritual relations" as a response to reduced mobility (pp. 95-96)
- Social networks replace residential movement as the primary mechanism for coping with resource uncertainty during sedentarization (pp. 93-94)
- Gregory, Davis, and Hunter (1970) proposed "seasonal production with the maintenance of a large nucleated center, Poverty Point proper, by in and out moving groups" (p. 28)
- Gibson (1973) noted all known Poverty Point sites are "situated at the edges of two or more ecotones" (pp. 36-37 via Ortmann)

### From Hays 2018
- "The archaeological data do not support the model that a sizable, single ethnic community continuously resided at Poverty Point" (p. 4)
- Jackson (1991b) argued "available data on foods consumed by local populations in the Poverty Point area indicate that it is more likely that the site was occupied periodically rather than continuously" (p. 4)
- Analysis of flora and fauna at nearby Copes "indicated no regional stress on subsistence resources. Indeed the Copes residents were highly selective in their subsistence pursuits, which would not occur if there was a large resident population" (p. 4)
- The area "is rich in plant and animal resources but it is essentially a stoneless land with the closest gravels suitable for use over 30 km away" (p. 1)
- Poverty Point's "massive earthworks and its extraordinarily large and diverse artifact assemblages make sense if there were large numbers of people from different ethnic groups coming together at the site" (p. 4)

### From Haag and Webb 1953
- Intra-site spatial patterning: microblade production concentrated along earthwork ridges; cooking activities (clay ball concentrations in dark soil) in separate areas; some areas have both together (pp. 245-246)
- Webb interprets these "differential frequencies" as reflecting "the living and occupational habits of the people instead of a difference in relative age" (p. 246)
- The microflint industry produced "several thousands" of blades at Jaketown and "nearly a thousand" from surface collection at Poverty Point in just a few visits (p. 246)
- Poverty Point Plantation represented "prolonged occupation at this site by a comparatively large population" (p. 247)

### From Gagliano and Saucier 1963
- Bayou Jasmine and Linsley sites were "small earth and Rangia shell middens not exceeding 150 feet in greatest dimension and 2 to 3 feet in thickness" (p. 321)
- Linsley site faunal remains: alligators, alligator gars, rabbits, squirrels, otters, raccoons, muskrats, deer, and "a wide variety of fish and birds" indicating broad-spectrum hunting and gathering in a wetland environment (p. 321)
- "A complete absence of human skeletal remains and the presence of large quantities of animal bone suggest a specialized and perhaps seasonal occupation oriented toward hunting and gathering in a paludal environment" (p. 326)
- The Bayou Jasmine phase represents "small Rangia shell and earth middens associated with distributaries of an early (4000-year-old) Mississippi River subdelta" (p. 326)

### From Byrd 1991
- Jackson's Copes site analysis: "plant procurement centered on nut resources"; pecans occur in 74% of samples, acorns in 52%; "pecans, fish, and deer provided the backbone of the subsistence system" (pp. 133, 139, 149)
- Nut mast variability: "average yields about 30-45% maximum yields and poorest yields between 2 and 7%"; "synchronized high production of all oaks probably occurs no more often than once in every 10 years" (pp. 144-145)
- During poor nut production years, "alternative collecting areas, a shift to less nutritious, 'second line' resources, or reliance on the productive capacity of other social groups would be required" (p. 145)
- "The highly selective nature of plant resource use is relevant in regards to previous interpretations of the Poverty Point system. Such a strategy seems possible only under conditions of low regional population density" (p. 149)
- "The independence of the Copes site community, at least with respect to subsistence provisioning, implies that if the Poverty Point phase represents a chiefdom, it did not serve a redistributive role" (p. 150)
- Fishing was "the key to the evolution of a basically sedentary settlement system in the first place" (p. 149)
- Multiple seasonal indicators at Copes: "early spring (March) through late fall (November or December). Evidence for mid-winter occupation of the site is weakest" (p. 139)

## Synthesis

The subsistence evidence consistently indicates that Poverty Point populations were hunter-gatherers exploiting a diverse resource base dominated by fish, deer, and tree nuts (hickory, pecan, acorn), with no significant agricultural component. Systematic flotation at the type-site produced negligible evidence for cultigens: only single specimens of Polygonum and Chenopodium. The only possible domesticate, squash, comes from a context with potential contamination from later occupations. This non-agricultural economic base is foundational to the costly signaling argument, as it means the labor invested in monumental construction and long-distance exchange was diverted from foraging rather than drawn from agricultural surplus.

The question of seasonal versus permanent occupation remains incompletely resolved but the weight of evidence favors seasonal aggregation. Jackson's analysis at the Copes site suggests near year-round occupation of peripheral sites, but he explicitly argues that a permanent population of substantial size at Poverty Point itself would have caused environmental degradation. The artifact diversity at Poverty Point (multiple distinct projectile point traditions, all six PPO types from the lowest to uppermost levels) is consistent with multiple groups converging at the site rather than a single resident community. Hays (2018) argues the data "do not support the model that a sizable, single ethnic community continuously resided at Poverty Point."

The environmental setting is critical to understanding why aggregation occurred at this specific location. Every documented Poverty Point site occupies an ecotone position providing access to multiple ecological zones. The Poverty Point type-site sits at the junction of Macon Ridge uplands and Mississippi floodplain, with access to six or seven major rivers during its occupation. This ecotone positioning maximized resource diversity and stability, reducing the risk that seasonal aggregation would fail catastrophically. The high variability in nut mast production (average yields only 30-45% of maximum; synchronized good years occurring perhaps once per decade) created recurrent resource uncertainty that made inter-group cooperation and risk-pooling adaptive.

## Gaps
- No direct faunal data exist from the Poverty Point type-site itself due to poor organic preservation in loess soils
- Seasonality of occupation at Poverty Point proper has not been directly tested (all seasonal indicators come from satellite sites)
- Population estimates for the aggregation events range widely (Gibson's 4,000-5,000 to Ortmann and Kidder's 1,000-3,000 laborers)
- The frequency and duration of aggregation events is unknown
- Whether different portions of the site were occupied by different groups has not been systematically tested
- The role of communal fishing (e.g., fish weirs) as a driver of aggregation is hypothesized but not demonstrated
