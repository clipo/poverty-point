# Exchange Networks Evidence

## Key Questions
- What was the geographic extent and structure of the Poverty Point exchange network?
- What materials were transported, from what sources, and in what quantities?
- Did exchange patterns change over time, and what mechanisms drove those changes?

## Evidence

### From Ford and Webb 1956 (Webb 1956)
- Projectile points made from Harrison County, Indiana chert; Zaleski flint from Ohio; and ball flint from Union County, Illinois document lithic procurement over 500-700+ airline miles (p. 51)
- A steatite vessel cache of 2,205 fragments plus 519 surface finds was geologically sourced to the southern Appalachians, approximately 900+ km distant (p. 107)
- Fourteen galena fragments found, with sources attributed to southern Missouri, approximately 800 km distant; one large nodule weighed 7 pounds (p. 104)
- A copper plummet (48 mm, perforated) of native copper, likely hammered from a nugget, represents a Great Lakes source approximately 1,600 km away (p. 115)
- Four quartz crystals from Arkansas near Hot Springs, approximately 300 km distant (p. 104)
- Substantial fire-cracked imported sandstone alongside clay cooking balls indicates transport of stone from at least 30 miles away for utilitarian purposes (p. 39)

### From Webb 1968
- By 1968, 34 definite Poverty Point sites were identified across Louisiana, Mississippi, and Arkansas, with related sites extending from Indiana to Florida and Georgia (p. 304, Table 1)
- At the Garcia site on eastern Lake Pontchartrain, "a high percentage of materials is imported; these include quartz, novaculite, metamorphic rocks, magnetite, and hematite" (p. 298)
- At the Kelly site on the Ohio River in Indiana, approximately 900 km from Poverty Point, several hundred baked clay balls of Poverty Point type were documented alongside hematite plummets, galena, red jasper beads, and cannel coal (pp. 307-308)
- A cache of 43 polished hematite perforated plummets was found at the Lea site on the Ouachita River (p. 302)
- Approximately 70,000 artifacts were studied from the type-site alone (p. 297)

### From Webb 1982
- More than 100 Poverty Point sites and an equal number of related sites were known by 1982 (p. 5)
- All Poverty Point sites occupy "contact zones, which were strategic ecotones that permitted exploitation of combinations of upland forest, lowland swamp, riverine, lacustrine, or paludal environments" (p. 5)
- Outer ridge diameter is 1,208 m; total ridge construction length is approximately 11.2 km (p. 9)

### From Bell 1956
- First reported copper artifact from Poverty Point: a native copper plummet, 48 mm long, 20 mm diameter, perforated for suspension, found near Mound A (p. 80)
- Copper was "unreported for the Poverty Point site" before this find, establishing the earliest evidence for Great Lakes copper at the site (p. 80)

### From Blackburn 2003
- Non-local materials at Poverty Point sites include "galena or lead ore, jasper, greenstone, hematite, magnetite, steatite, granite, as well as clear silica or quartz crystals" (p. 24)
- Hematite hardness of 5.5-6.5 on the Mohs scale makes these plummets genuinely costly to produce (p. 24)
- Drilled/perforated plummets are "almost exclusively fashioned from magnetite, while the grooved plummets are almost exclusively made from hematite" (p. 26)
- Poverty Point-style plummets found outside the core distribution zone in Christian County, Kentucky, indicating exchange network spread (pp. 25-26)

### From Hill et al. 2010
- LA-ICP-MS analysis of six copper artifacts: over 65% match Nova Scotia sources more closely than Lake Superior, challenging the long-held assumption of a single Great Lakes origin (pp. 3-5)
- At least some copper is inconsistent with known Great Lakes sources, suggesting a more complex exchange network (p. 5)
- Multiple source regions may be represented, including Canadian Maritime sources potentially 2,500+ km distant (pp. 3-4)
- Comparative database limitation: southern Appalachian copper sources were not included, leaving closer sources (~800-900 km) as an untested alternative (p. 5)

### From Lehmann 1990
- Table 2 documents foreign lithics from six source regions across 28 Yazoo Basin sites, with tiered access patterns suggesting differential participation in the exchange network (p. 127)
- Gibson's temporal analysis shows shifting source exploitation at Poverty Point: early emphasis on Ouachita/Tennessee River sources, addition of Great Lakes copper, and eventual shift to midwestern sources (p. 128)
- Poverty Point populations continued using costly steatite vessels despite knowing ceramic technology, a deliberate choice to bear higher costs for exotic materials (p. 128)
- Mississippi River meander shift (ca. 650 B.C.) may have severed the Yazoo Basin's direct water route to Tennessee River sources, providing a physical mechanism for exchange network disruption (p. 128)

### From Gagliano and Saucier 1963
- Poverty Point cultural sphere extended to deltaic lowlands of southeastern Louisiana with sites at Bayou Jasmine, Linsley, and Garcia in the Pontchartrain Basin (pp. 320-321)
- Garcia site contained "quartz crystals, novaculite, orthoquartzite, metamorphic rocks, magnetite and hematite" from Arkansas, Missouri, and the Appalachians/Piedmont (p. 326)
- Bayou Jasmine phase radiocarbon date of ca. 1740 B.C. indicates early participation in the Poverty Point exchange system (p. 326)

### From Byrd 1991
- Neutron activation analysis confirms southern Appalachian quarries (North Carolina, Georgia, Alabama) as steatite sources, approximately 800-900 km distant (p. 177)
- 95.7% of all known steatite (6,081 of 6,352 sherds) concentrates at the Poverty Point site itself (pp. 173-174)
- 97% of all known galena (702 of 724 cubes/modules) comes from Poverty Point, indicating extreme concentration rather than redistribution (p. 177)
- "Although the Poverty Point site clearly functioned as a regional distribution center for some exotic lithic materials, little galena or steatite was distributed to other sites" (p. 177)
- Non-local lithic materials traded include over 20 material types from multiple distant sources (p. 173)
- Exchange crossed "ethnic boundaries and probably language boundaries" among groups that were "strangers" to one another (p. 196)

### From Sassaman 2005
- Soapstone came primarily from the southern Piedmont of west Georgia and east Alabama, over 600 km away, and "the direct route between soapstone sources and Poverty Point was not used" (p. 353)
- Claiborne on the Mississippi Gulf coast is "a one-sixth model of Poverty Point in reverse," suggesting architectural forms carried symbolic value across the network (p. 354)
- Nested spatial structure: core (4 km), community periphery (33 km), distant communities (200+ km), and remote affiliates (600+ km) (p. 346)

### From Hill, Greenlee & Neff 2016

- LA-ICP-MS analysis of six PP copper artifacts (~3.2% of the known PP copper assemblage of ~188 objects) compared against 62 native-copper source samples from Lake Superior, Maritime, and Appalachian source regions. All six PP samples assigned to eastern (Appalachian / Maritime) sources rather than Lake Superior across three discriminant function tests (Abstract; pp. 9-11). Overturns the longstanding archaeological assumption that PP copper came from the Great Lakes.
- Two-region test (Lake Superior vs. all eastern sources): 100% of PP samples to eastern sources, probabilities 0.73-0.97 (p. 10). Three-region test: 4/6 Maritimes (0.56-0.80), 2/6 Appalachians (0.68-0.69) (p. 10). Four-region test: all six samples assigned to Nova Scotia (0.54-0.99) (pp. 10-11).
- Authors flag the Nova Scotia clustering as possibly reflecting undersampling of southern Appalachian source rocks rather than actual Maritime provenance: "the surprising similarities with copper sources of Nova Scotia are logically consistent with potential unsampled areas in the Appalachians" (p. 15).
- Authors explicitly maintain methodological caution: "The current study cannot rule out the use of Lake Superior copper at Poverty Point, rather it can only demonstrate that there is little indication of such source utilization in the samples examined here" (p. 16).
- Important distinction from galena: PP galena is documented to come from Upper Mississippi Valley sources (Walthall et al. 1982; Ghosh 2008); PP copper does not. The Mississippi-River-transport assumption that linked galena and copper as following the same northern pathway is undercut by the eastern-source finding for copper (p. 15).
- Speculative implication for Claiborne and Cedarland: the eastern-source finding suggests a potential coastal transport pathway from southern Appalachians via the Pearl River sites to PP, with implications for the direction of regional redistribution (p. 15). The qualitative finding (PP copper is from a geologically constrained, geographically distant source ~800-1600 km away) is unchanged regardless of the specific source-region attribution. **This 2016 published version supersedes the unpublished 2010 SAA symposium-paper precursor (`hill_etal_2010` in INDEX), which reported the same six-sample analysis at preliminary stage and is marked "DO NOT CITE WITHOUT PERMISSION."**

## Synthesis

The Poverty Point exchange network was one of the most extensive in pre-Columbian North America, connecting populations across a geographic range of at least 1,600 km (and possibly 2,500+ km if Canadian Maritime copper sources are confirmed). Materials moved along multiple directional pathways: steatite from the southern Appalachians (~900 km), galena from Missouri (~800 km), copper from the Great Lakes or possibly farther (~1,600+ km), novaculite from the Ouachita Mountains (~250 km), and various cherts from Indiana, Ohio, and Illinois (~500-900 km). The network was not static; Gibson's analysis documents shifting source exploitation over the site's approximately 600-year occupation, and geomorphological changes (Mississippi River meander shifts) physically disrupted some exchange connections.

A striking pattern emerges from the distributional data: exotic materials overwhelmingly concentrate at the Poverty Point site itself rather than being redistributed outward. Steatite (95.7%), galena (97%), and plummets are all massively concentrated at the type-site, with only small quantities reaching satellite communities. This concentration is inconsistent with a redistributive chiefdom model but consistent with a signaling/aggregation framework where materials were brought to the center for display or ritual deposition. The observation that "metric tons" of material flow into Poverty Point with "nothing visible" going out reinforces this interpretation.

The exchange network included both finished goods and raw materials. Unworked minerals (magnetite nodules, galena cubes, quartz crystals, raw ocher) arrived at Poverty Point alongside finished items (steatite vessels, jasper beads, copper ornaments). This dual character suggests materials were valued both as raw substances and as finished display objects. The continued preference for costly imported steatite vessels over cheaper locally producible ceramics, despite knowledge of pottery technology, provides direct evidence that acquisition cost itself was part of the value, precisely the dynamic predicted by costly signaling theory.

## Gaps
- Geochemical sourcing of copper remains preliminary (n=6 artifacts, southern Appalachian sources untested)
- No systematic sourcing study exists for hematite and magnetite plummets
- The structure of exchange (direct acquisition vs. down-the-line vs. intermediary trade) remains uncertain
- Perishable trade goods (hides, textiles, feathers, salt, food) are invisible archaeologically but may have constituted the majority of exchange volume
- Quantitative transport cost estimates per material type are not available
- The chronological resolution of exchange network expansion and contraction is poorly constrained
